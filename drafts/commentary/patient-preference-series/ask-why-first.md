# Ask Why First

**Dr. Naomi Whittaker, MD, Board-Certified OBGYN, MIGS, NFPMC, FCI**

A modest, diagnostic approach to infertility became a political controversy. That is worth pausing on.

The New York Times ran a piece on restorative reproductive medicine. MedPage Today weighed in. Fertility trade journals joined the response. The reaction was striking in its intensity, for what is, at its core, a proposal to find the cause before starting treatment. That is not a fringe position. In every other field of medicine, it is called a workup.

What provoked all of this attention? Patients are asking a reasonable question. They want to know why before they are sent to a lab.

## What patients actually reported

A nationally representative survey published in 2026 in the Journal of Restorative Reproductive Medicine asked U.S. adults about their preferences in fertility care. Two independent polling firms ran separate surveys: J.L. Partners (N=1,002) and McLaughlin and Associates (N=1,000). The consistency across both was striking.

Seventy percent of respondents wanted treatments that address the underlying causes of infertility. Not seventy percent of NaProTechnology patients. Not a self-selected advocacy sample. Seventy percent of a demographically representative national cross-section.

IVF patients specifically reported concern about undiagnosed health issues and about being rushed into IVF. These are the words of people who went through the process, not people who declined it.

That is not ideology. That is a clinical complaint. And it points directly to a gap in the standard of care.

## The diagnostic question that keeps getting skipped

The same medical system that demands a biopsy before treating a suspicious lesion, a stress test before ruling out cardiac disease, a lumbar MRI before spinal surgery, has somehow normalized moving a couple toward a $15,000-plus procedure without answering the most basic clinical question: why aren't they conceiving?

IVF is not a diagnostic intervention. It does not identify the cause of infertility. It routes around it. A woman may have undiagnosed endometriosis, a progesterone deficiency timed to her cycle, a structural issue amenable to surgical repair, a partner with an easily correctable sperm morphology problem. IVF does not find any of those things. It bypasses them. And if she becomes pregnant through IVF, those conditions remain.

The survey respondents who reported concern about undiagnosed health issues were not wrong. They were clinically right.

## Cause-first is ordinary medicine

The fertility establishment has framed diagnosis-first care as a niche religious position. It is not. It is how medicine is supposed to work.

When a patient presents with chest pain, the goal is a diagnosis. When a patient presents with unexplained weight loss, the goal is a diagnosis. When a couple presents with infertility, the same logic applies. There is a reason they are not conceiving. That reason is, in most cases, findable. Finding it and treating it is not alternative medicine. It is medicine.

The survey data make this concrete. When respondents learned about both approaches, 69% preferred fertilization in the woman's body over fertilization in a lab. Initial support for IVF was approximately 80%, but that support dropped roughly 10 percentage points after people learned what IVF actually involves: the creation and potential non-use of embryos, the medical risks, the absence of a diagnosis, and the government oversight questions. Nearly half of respondents were unaware of any medical risks of IVF before reading about them.

This is not a persuasion gap that favors IVF. It is a knowledge gap. When people understand both options, their preferences shift toward the one that answers the question first.

## What patients rank highest

The same survey asked what matters most in fertility care. Baby health ranked first, at 74% of respondents. Cost ranked at 13%. Time to conceive ranked at 6%.

This is worth pausing on. The fertility industry has organized itself largely around time to conceive. Speed is positioned as a selling point, a competitive advantage, the reason to act now. But the people being served rank it last. They want a healthy child. And they want to know whether they have an underlying condition that needs to be found and treated, including for the health of any pregnancy that follows.

Couple-centered evaluation matters here, too. Male factor is solely responsible in approximately 20% of cases presenting with infertility, and contributory in a much larger fraction beyond that. A diagnosis-first approach evaluates both partners from the first visit. That is not a procedural courtesy. It is clinical accuracy.

## The reaction and what it reveals

A paradigm secure in its clinical grounding does not need to preemptively label its diagnostic alternatives as fringe. The intensity of the response to restorative reproductive medicine tells us something.

Patients are telling us something, too. They want to know why. They want to know if there is a treatable condition behind their infertility. They want care that cooperates with their physiology rather than routing around it. And when given accurate information about both approaches, they prefer the one that begins with a question rather than a procedure.

That is ordinary medicine. It should not be controversial.
