# IVF Has the Public's Support. Not Its Informed Consent.

*Dr. Naomi Whittaker, MD, Board-Certified OBGYN, MIGS, NFPMC, FCI*

Eighty percent of Americans say they support IVF. That number is cited constantly, deployed with confidence, treated as settled cultural consensus. Here is the part that rarely appears in the same sentence: nearly half of those same respondents could not name a single medical risk of the procedure they were endorsing. When risks were disclosed, support dropped by roughly ten percentage points. And when people were told there was an alternative approach that identifies and treats the causes of infertility rather than routing around them, 70 percent wanted that.

That is not the profile of an informed public. That is the profile of a public that has absorbed a default position inside an information vacuum.

A recent secondary analysis of two independent, nationally representative U.S. surveys, published in the Journal of Restorative Reproductive Medicine (Parnell, Copeland, and Minjeur, 2026), documents this gap with precision. Two firms, J.L. Partners and McLaughlin and Associates, surveyed a combined two thousand adults. Their findings were consistent across both samples. The numbers do not describe a population that has weighed fertility options and chosen IVF. They describe a population that knows very little about fertility medicine at all and has absorbed a default preference that the culture handed them.

When those survey findings surfaced, the coverage followed a familiar script. The New York Times wrote it up. MedPage Today weighed in. Fertility trade journals joined the response. Restorative reproductive medicine, the story went, is fringe, religiously motivated, or a political threat to IVF access. Look carefully at the data, and that script falls apart. The public's concerns are not religious. They are practical and medical.

Seventy percent of respondents said they wanted treatments that address the underlying causes of infertility. That is a clinical preference, not a theological one. Patient priorities placed baby health at 74 percent, far above cost at 13 percent and time to conceive at 6 percent. IVF patients specifically reported concern about undiagnosed health issues and being rushed toward a procedure before a diagnosis was established. These are the words of people who wanted more medicine, not less. More information, not ideology.

Forty-three percent of respondents had never heard of restorative reproductive medicine. That number is the crux of the problem. When nearly half the population has never encountered the alternative, what the surveys are measuring is not a preference. It is a gap. A choice made inside an information vacuum is not a free choice. It is a default.

Informed consent is one of the oldest principles in medicine. It means a patient understands what a procedure involves, what it does not do, what the alternatives are, and what the evidence actually shows. It is not a form. It is a clinical responsibility. In most areas of medicine, this standard is enforced by professional norms, by litigation risk, by training programs that drill it into residents before they ever touch a patient.

Fertility care is the one area where that standard reliably thins out.

Patients arrive at IVF consultations having absorbed a cultural message: the body has failed, technology will compensate. What they often do not receive is a workup for the reason their body appears to be failing. They are not told that "unexplained infertility" is frequently explicable when a clinician actually looks for a cause. They are not told about the medical risks in any systematic way: the stimulation protocols, the outcomes for embryos that do not survive the process, the pregnancy complications associated with IVF-conceived pregnancies. They are told about success rates in ways that, these surveys suggest, are not landing with any clarity.

IVF is not a treatment for the underlying disease. It routes around the problem. A couple with endometriosis, a luteal phase defect, a structural tubal issue, or a male factor that has never been properly evaluated does not have those conditions corrected by retrieving eggs and fertilizing them outside the body. The underlying pathology remains. In many cases, it progresses.

The survey data points toward something the fertility establishment should reckon with rather than deflect. After both approaches were described, 69 percent of respondents preferred natural fertilization in the woman's body compared to 17 percent who preferred lab fertilization. Both surveys showed strong, consistent support for full patient access to information about treatments and processes. The public is not asking for less medicine. It is asking for honest medicine.

Restorative reproductive medicine starts with the diagnostic question that too many fertility consultations skip. What is causing this? Not: how do we work around it? The answers to that question often exist. Endometriosis missed for years on suppressive medications. Thyroid dysfunction never screened. A semen analysis that was never run, or run once and filed away. Male factor is the primary driver in approximately one in five couples facing infertility and a contributing factor in many more. A field that treats infertility as the woman's problem alone is, from the start, practicing with incomplete data.

Eighty percent support for IVF, narrowed by ten points on disclosure, with 43 percent of the population having never heard of the alternative. That is a consent gap. It is measurable, it is documented, and it exists because one side of the information landscape has been systematically underfunded, undersupported, and until recently, largely invisible.

The question for clinicians and for policymakers is not whether patients support IVF. The question is whether that support was freely given, after full disclosure, with real knowledge of what else is possible. The surveys answer that question. Support given without information is not consent. It is exposure.

More information moves people toward diagnosis-first care. That is a clinical finding.
