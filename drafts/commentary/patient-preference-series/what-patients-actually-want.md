# What Patients Actually Want

**Dr. Naomi Whittaker, MD, Board-Certified OBGYN, MIGS, NFPMC, FCI**

*The fertility industry competes on speed and success rates. Two national surveys reveal that patients rank baby health first, by a landslide, and the dominant model is optimizing for the wrong thing.*

---

Ask a room of couples struggling to conceive what matters most to them. Not what they've been told to want. Not what the billboards promise. What they actually rank, in order, if forced to choose.

Two independent, nationally representative surveys asked exactly that. The answer: baby health first, at 74%. Cost came in at 13%. Time to conceive was 6%.

That ranking deserves a second look. Baby health. Then cost. Then speed. By a margin that isn't close.

Now look at how mainstream fertility care markets itself. Cycle counts. Success rates per retrieval. Expedited timelines. The dominant pitch is speed and probability, dressed in the language of hope. It is not built around the health of the baby. It is built around the transaction.

The mismatch is not small.

---

## What two national surveys actually found

The Parnell, Copeland, and Minjeur 2026 study, published in the Journal of Restorative Reproductive Medicine, analyzed secondary data from two independent surveys: J.L. Partners (1,002 respondents) and McLaughlin & Associates (1,000 respondents). Both were nationally representative of U.S. adults. Both produced results that were strikingly consistent with each other.

The priority ranking: 74% placed baby health first. Far behind were cost at 13% and time to conceive at 6%.

Seventy percent of respondents wanted treatments that address the underlying causes of infertility. Not symptom management. Not a technological workaround. The root cause.

On IVF specifically: initial support ran around 80%. But most of those respondents had limited knowledge of what IVF actually involves. Nearly half were unaware of any medical risks. When they were fully informed, including medical risks, embryo creation and use, and preimplantation genetic testing, support dropped by roughly 10 percentage points. Knowledge changed minds.

On restorative reproductive medicine: 33% expressed initial support, and 43% had never heard of it. After learning about both approaches, 69% preferred fertilization in the woman's body over fertilization in a lab. The preference wasn't close either.

IVF patients surveyed specifically named two concerns: undiagnosed health issues, and feeling rushed into IVF. Those are not fringe concerns. They are the center of what patients say went wrong.

## The reaction, and what it reveals

Over the past year, a wave of commentary from fertility establishment voices and mainstream press has described restorative reproductive medicine as fringe, religiously motivated, or a political threat to IVF access. The New York Times covered it. MedPage Today weighed in. Fertility trade journals joined in. They share a common move: frame a diagnostic, cause-first approach as dangerous or marginal.

The intensity of the reaction is worth examining. An approach built on evaluation, root-cause diagnosis, and cooperating with physiology rather than bypassing it does not normally provoke outsized responses. When it does, the question worth asking is what the reaction is defending.

The surveys above offer a simpler explanation. When patients are informed, they don't necessarily choose what the establishment expects them to choose. That is not a threat to access. That is informed consent working as designed.

---

## What the dominant model is optimizing for

IVF is not a treatment for infertility's causes. It routes around them. The sperm and egg are removed from the body, combined outside it, and the resulting embryo is transferred back in. Any undiagnosed condition that caused the infertility, whether endometriosis, a luteal phase defect, a sperm problem, or an anatomical issue, remains undiagnosed. The next cycle of IVF faces the same underlying problem.

This is not a criticism of individual clinicians. Most are doing exactly what their training equipped them to do. It is a structural observation: a system that bills per cycle and measures success per retrieval has incentives that do not align cleanly with identifying a root cause that might make further cycles unnecessary.

The 74% who rank baby health first are not asking for IVF. They are asking for an answer.

---

## What restorative care is optimizing for

Restorative reproductive medicine starts with a different question. Not "how do we achieve a pregnancy despite the problem?" but "what is causing this, and can we address it?"

That question is not a political position or a religious one. It is the question 70% of respondents in these surveys said they want their care to center on. The surveys didn't produce it. They found it already there, waiting to be named.

Restorative clinicians evaluate both partners, because male factor is solely responsible in roughly one in five cases and contributory in many more. They look for diagnosable conditions before recommending any intervention. They work with the couple's physiology rather than substituting for it. When underlying disease is found and treated, conception may follow in the body, at lower cost, with lower medical burden on the mother.

The surveys show that 69% of respondents, once informed, preferred that path. Not as a second choice. As a first choice.

---

## Health-first fertility care

Fertility care organized around baby health does not optimize for speed. It optimizes for diagnosis. A couple who receives an accurate diagnosis, whose treatable conditions are addressed, and who conceives naturally, has received something the speed-and-success-rate model rarely promises: an answer.

The surveys didn't reveal a fringe preference. They revealed the mainstream one. Patients want their baby to be healthy. They want to know what is wrong. They want treatments that address the cause.

That is what patients came in asking for. It is what restorative reproductive medicine is built to provide.
