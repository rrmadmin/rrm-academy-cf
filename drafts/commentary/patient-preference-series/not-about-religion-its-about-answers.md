# It Isn't About Religion. It's About Answers.

**Dr. Naomi Whittaker, MD, Board-Certified OBGYN, MIGS, NFPMC, FCI**

---

Start with what the public actually said.

When two independent, nationally representative surveys asked American adults whether they preferred a baby conceived naturally in the mother's body versus conceived in a laboratory, 69% chose in the body. Seventeen percent chose the lab. When asked whether they wanted treatments that address the underlying causes of infertility rather than route around them, 70% said yes.

These are not fringe numbers from a sympathetic audience. They come from polls conducted by J.L. Partners (N=1,002) and McLaughlin & Associates (N=1,000), run independently of the researchers, with populations reflecting U.S. adults broadly. The results were consistent across both surveys.

That is the foundation for everything that follows.

---

Over the past year or so, a genre of commentary has developed in the fertility establishment and the press. The New York Times covered restorative reproductive medicine. MedPage Today covered it. Fertility trade journals weighed in. The framing, across most of this coverage, runs something like this: restorative reproductive medicine is religiously motivated, politically entangled, fringe, and perhaps a threat to IVF access.

The reaction is outsized for what RRM actually is: a diagnostic-first approach to reproductive health that asks why fertility is disrupted before deciding what to do about it. The principle is straightforward. The response has not been.

I want to name what is happening here without spending too much time on it, because the more interesting story is what the data shows. The genre has miscategorized a clinical orientation as a religious one. RRM does have a presence in Catholic medical communities, and some of its methods were developed within that tradition. But the inference runs backward. The fact that a medical approach is compatible with a set of values does not make it exclusively the property of that set of values. Cause-based reproductive care is compatible with many worldviews. What the surveys reveal is that it is compatible with the mainstream.

---

Before the surveys taught people what RRM is, 33% supported it. That is already meaningful for a medical approach most people had never encountered. But here is the more telling number: 43% of respondents had never heard of RRM at all.

That is the actual barrier. Not doctrine. Awareness.

IVF, by contrast, is broadly known and broadly supported at the outset. Around 80% of respondents expressed initial support. But that support was built on limited knowledge. Nearly half were unaware of any medical risks of IVF. When the surveys disclosed those risks, along with facts about embryo creation, preimplantation genetic testing, and the absence of government oversight, stated support for IVF fell by roughly 10 percentage points.

The surveys did not ask people to oppose IVF. They asked people to understand both approaches and state a preference. Once informed, 69% chose in-body conception. Seventeen percent chose the lab. The gap is not small.

This is what an awareness gap looks like when you close it.

---

IVF routes around the problem. It does not diagnose the problem. It does not restore function. A woman who conceives through IVF still has the endometriosis, the luteal phase defect, the tubal factor, or whatever condition drove the infertility. The disease is still there. The cycle is still disrupted. The question that wasn't asked is still unanswered.

Seventy percent of the people in these surveys wanted the question asked. They wanted the underlying cause addressed. That is not a religious preference. That is a clinical preference, and a reasonable one.

IVF patients themselves, in these surveys, reported concern about undiagnosed health issues. They reported feeling rushed into the procedure. These are patients who chose IVF and still wanted more. They wanted to know why.

---

What do people actually prioritize when it comes to fertility care? The surveys asked. Baby health ranked first, at 74%. Cost came in at 13%. Time to conceive came in at 6%.

This is worth sitting with. The fertility establishment has spent decades optimizing for time to conceive. IVF is fast. That speed is part of its appeal, particularly when couples have already waited years for a diagnosis. But patients are not primarily asking for speed. They are asking for a healthy baby and, by extension, a thorough understanding of what is happening in their bodies.

These are not values that require any particular religion to hold.

---

The genre of commentary I described earlier has done the public a disservice. The disservice is in the category error. Casting RRM as a religious-political project rather than a clinical paradigm forecloses a public conversation that the public is clearly ready to have.

The surveys show a public that is curious, underinformed, and, once informed, broadly sympathetic to a cause-first approach. The barrier is not preference. Forty-three percent of Americans have not heard of this. When they do hear of it, and understand what it actually involves, the numbers shift substantially.

The strong consistency across two independent pollsters is not a niche result. It is a signal about what people want from medicine when they are given the choice.

---

This approach is medicine. It asks: why is this happening? It looks for an answer. It addresses what it finds. The fact that some people practice it within a faith framework does not make it doctrinal. Cause-based care does not belong to any tradition. It belongs to the practice of medicine at its most basic.

The public seems to know that. The question is whether the institutions that shape fertility care are willing to listen.
