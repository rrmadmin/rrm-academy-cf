## The call that comes a week later

Fifteen eggs. Everyone was pleased. The nurse used the word excellent, and for a few days it felt like the hard part was over.

Then the calls start. Eleven were mature. Eight fertilized. Five were still growing on day three. Two made it to blastocyst. One was normal on testing.

By the end of that week, a woman who was told she had responded beautifully is sitting with one embryo and a feeling she cannot name, which usually turns into a question she asks quietly and then apologizes for asking. What did I do wrong?

Nothing. This is what the procedure does. And she was not told.

## The number that answers her question

The number a patient is quoted before she starts is almost always a success rate per cycle or per transfer. That number answers a question the clinic needs answered, which is how often a started cycle or a completed transfer ends in a birth.

It does not answer the question she is actually asking, which is what happened to her eggs.

There is a published number for that. Researchers at a single academic fertility program reviewed every autologous cycle from 2014 through 2020: 12,717 patients, 20,677 retrievals, 248,004 eggs, 57,268 embryos. Among the patients who had used all of their embryos, the live birth rate per egg retrieved was **2.82 percent**. By age band, it ran from 11.3 percent in women under 35 down to 1.2 percent in women over 42. For patients who still had embryos in storage and had used genetic testing, the projected figure was 8.34 percent.

That is Sabbagh and colleagues, in Fertility and Sterility, the flagship journal of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine. The field counted its own work and published the result.

Across all ages, roughly one egg in thirty-five became a baby.

## Both numbers are true

A clinic quoting a live birth rate per transfer and a paper reporting 2.82 percent per egg are not contradicting each other. They are counting different things.

Per transfer counts only the women who got as far as a transfer. Per cycle counts the women who started. Per egg counts every egg that was retrieved from a woman's body under anesthesia at the end of a stimulation cycle.

Each denominator is legitimate. Each answers a different question. Unfortunately, the one that describes the woman's own experience is the one she is least likely to be shown, and she is generally not told that a choice of denominator was made at all.

She is entitled to both. She is entitled to be told, before the first injection, that fifteen eggs and one embryo is not an unusual outcome or a sign that her body failed. It is the shape of the thing.

## Where the eggs go

The losses are not mysterious, and they are not anyone's fault.

Some eggs retrieved are immature and cannot be used. Some mature eggs do not fertilize. Some fertilized eggs arrest in the first few days and stop dividing. Of those that reach the blastocyst stage, a share carry chromosomal errors that make a birth impossible. Each step removes some.

Women in this process feel that attrition day by day. In the IVF community they call the embryos embabies, and they are on the phone every morning finding out how many are still there. Nobody prepared them for that either.

## What this changes

There is a version of this conversation that is only demoralizing, and that is not the point of writing it down.

The point is that a woman deciding whether to start ovarian stimulation deserves the arithmetic in advance. Not "most people need more than one cycle." The actual number, for her age, from the clinic she is sitting in.

It changes what she asks. It changes how many cycles she plans and budgets for. For some women it changes whether the first step is stimulation at all, or whether the first step is finding out why she is not conceiving.

That second question is the one that tends to get skipped. A woman arrives after months or years of a cycle that has been telling her something, often with pain she was told to expect, sometimes with a chart nobody read, and the work-up runs long enough to establish that she is infertile without ever establishing why. Ovarian stimulation does not answer why. It goes around it.

Endometriosis, [PCOS](/pcos/), thyroid and other endocrine disease, [chronic endometritis](/endometritis/), luteal phase deficiency, an [isthmocele](/isthmocele/) from a prior cesarean, tubal disease: these are diagnosable, and most of them are treatable. A woman who has one of them and is not told is spending her eggs on the wrong problem.

## The question to ask

If you are in a consult this week, ask this one, and write down the answer:

**"In women my age at this clinic, what is the live birth rate per egg retrieved?"**

If the answer is a per-transfer number, ask again. If the answer is that they do not calculate it that way, that is an answer too, and it is worth knowing that the published figure across all ages at a large academic program was 2.82 percent.

Ask what happens to the eggs at each step, and what the numbers were for the last hundred women in your age band. Ask what has been ruled out. Ask what is causing your infertility, and keep asking until someone gives you a name for it.

You are allowed to want the arithmetic before you sign. The consent form is supposed to be where you get it.
