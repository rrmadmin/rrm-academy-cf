## A question doctors stopped learning to answer

Today, only 4% of physicians have received any formal training in fertility awareness-based methods. Only 6% can correctly describe how effective those methods actually are. Family medicine residents report, on average, less than an hour of instruction on the topic across their entire training.

That gap looks like a modern blind spot. It is actually the opposite. Physicians have been trying to answer this exact question, is there a specific window in the cycle when a woman is fertile, for nearly 1,900 years. The question is not new. What is new, historically speaking, is how few doctors are taught the answer.

## Soranus of Ephesus, 2nd century AD

Soranus practiced in Rome under the emperors Trajan and Hadrian, in the early 2nd century AD, after training in Alexandria. His textbook, *Gynecology*, is the most complete surviving work of ancient reproductive medicine, and it contains something rarely credited to ancient medicine at all: an explicit fertile-window model.

In Book I, chapter 36, Soranus writes that "the best time for fruitful intercourse is when menstruation is ending and abating," reasoning that in the days before menstruation the uterus is "overburdened and in an unresponsive state... incapable of carrying on two motions contrary to each other, one for the excretion of material, the other for receiving."

He did not stop at describing when to conceive. He gave the mirror-image advice for the opposite goal, telling women who wanted to avoid pregnancy to abstain around and after menses, the same window, applied in reverse. That is a physician treating cycle timing as a variable that predictably shifts pregnancy odds in both directions, nearly two thousand years before anyone could explain why.

## What he got right, and what he never had

Soranus's real contribution is easy to understate: he rejected the idea that fertility is spread evenly across the cycle. That alone put him far ahead of centuries of medicine that followed him.

His mechanism was wrong. He had no concept of ovulation, the mammalian egg was not identified until 1827, so his "window" was inferred from a theory about the uterus's internal moisture and warmth, not built from anything a woman could observe in her own body. And his specific window, timed to the days right after menses, generalizes a pattern that is only true for shorter cycles, where early ovulation and several days of sperm survival can genuinely overlap with that window. Applied as a universal rule, it fails for most cycle lengths.

The deeper gap is not the error. It is what never existed at all: a real-time, self-observable sign. Soranus's model had to be taken on theoretical faith. Nothing in it could be checked day to day.

## The mechanism arrives, sixteen centuries later

Karl Ernst von Baer identified the mammalian ovum in 1827, the first time ovulation had a physical basis at all. It took until the 1920s for that basis to become a usable model: Kyusaku Ogino in Japan and Hermann Knaus in Austria, working independently, established that ovulation is fixed relative to the *next* period, roughly 12 to 16 days before it, not the last one. That single correction overturned the "right after menses" assumption that had stood, largely unchallenged, since Soranus.

Even then, the model was still statistical. It estimated a fertile window from cycle-length history. It still gave a woman no way to check, in real time, whether this particular cycle was following the pattern.

## The sign nobody had for 1,900 years

That changed with John and Evelyn Billings, working in Australia from 1953. They identified that cervical mucus changes character as estrogen rises before ovulation, becoming progressively more fertile in quality, and that a woman can learn to observe this directly. For the first time, the fertile window was not calculated or theorized. It was read, in real time, from a sign the body was already producing.

This is the piece Soranus's model was missing entirely, not a better theory, but an observable signal. [The Billings Ovulation Method](/billings-ovulation-method/) turned a nearly two-thousand-year-old question into something a woman could answer for herself, cycle by cycle.

## From observation to diagnosis

What followed was refinement, not reinvention. Josef Rötzer combined mucus observation with basal body temperature into [sympto-thermal methods](/sympto-thermal-method/), cross-checking two independent signs. Thomas Hilgers standardized mucus charting into the Creighton Model, then built [NaProTechnology](/naprotechnology/) on top of it, using the cycle chart as a diagnostic tool to identify and treat the specific cause of an abnormal cycle rather than only tracking it. The [Marquette Model](/marquette-model/) added home hormonal monitors as an objective adjunct, useful when mucus signs alone are ambiguous, particularly postpartum.

Each of these depends on the piece that took nineteen centuries to arrive: a woman's own, observable, physiological data.

## Where that leaves medicine today

The training numbers at the top of this post are not a footnote, they are the actual state of the field. Fehring's 2004 survey found only 4% of physicians had received formal FABM training. A Canadian study the same decade found only 3-6% could correctly describe how effective these methods are. More recent data on family medicine residents found 27% reported zero time spent on FABM education during their training, and 56% reported less than an hour.

Soranus did not have ovulation, estrogen, or a single observable sign, and he still built an explicit, bidirectional fertile-window model into mainstream medical teaching for centuries to follow. The tools that make his question fully answerable have existed for over fifty years. Most physicians practicing today were never taught them.

### Did ancient physicians understand ovulation?

No. Ovulation was not identified until 1827. Ancient physicians like Soranus of Ephesus (2nd century AD) recognized that fertility varies across the cycle and identified a specific fertile window, but their explanations were built on humoral theory about the uterus, not on any correct reproductive mechanism.

### Who first identified cervical mucus as a fertility sign?

John and Evelyn Billings, working in Australia beginning in 1953, identified that cervical mucus changes in response to rising estrogen before ovulation, and that a woman can learn to observe this change directly. This became the Billings Ovulation Method and is the basis for the mucus observation used in later sympto-thermal and Creighton Model charting.

### How many doctors are trained in fertility awareness-based methods today?

Survey data indicates only about 4% of physicians have received formal training in fertility awareness-based methods, and only 3-6% can correctly describe their effectiveness. Family medicine residents report an average of under one hour of FABM instruction across their training.
