## A phone call nobody scripts for

The retrieval went well. The embryos were biopsied. The plan, as she understood it, was that the lab would look at the chromosomes and tell her which one to transfer first.

The call says none of them.

There is no good way to receive that, and there is often no warning that it was a realistic outcome. The counseling before the cycle was about how many eggs she might make. The number that would have mattered was the chance that the whole cohort came back with nothing to transfer, and it is published, and it is large enough that she should have heard it.

## What the number is

In 2014, a group at Reproductive Medicine Associates of New Jersey reported on 15,169 consecutive embryo biopsies screened for chromosomal abnormalities. It is a large single-program description of embryo chromosomal status by maternal age.

The measure that matters most to a patient is what they called the no euploid embryo rate: the proportion of women whose entire cohort of tested embryos came back abnormal, leaving nothing to transfer.

- Ages 26 to 37: **2 to 6 percent**
- Age 42: **33 percent**
- Age 44: **53 percent**

Aneuploidy climbed steadily after age 26. Among women 23 and under it already exceeded 40 percent of individual embryos, which surprises people who assume very young eggs are uniformly normal. The lowest risk sat between 26 and 30, and after age 43 the overall risk stopped measurably changing, because by then it is close to its ceiling.

At 42, in other words, a woman going into an embryo biopsy had roughly a one in three chance that the answer would be none of them. At 44, closer to a coin flip.

## The real figure is higher than that

Read the study's design carefully, because it matters to the woman being counseled.

Those percentages were calculated among patients who had blastocysts available for biopsy. A woman whose eggs did not fertilize, or whose embryos arrested before day five, never entered that denominator at all. Her cycle also ended with nothing to transfer, and she is not in the 33 percent.

So 33 percent at 42 is the chance of striking out among women who got far enough to have embryos to test. The chance of a retrieval producing nothing transferable, counted from the day of the retrieval, is higher than the published figure, and how much higher depends on the clinic and on the woman.

## What stimulation can and cannot change

Ovarian stimulation increases the number of follicles that mature in a given cycle. Higher doses, different protocols, added growth hormone, and various supplements are aimed at that number.

None of that changes the chromosomal status of the eggs. The errors counted in that study happen during the egg's own division, and they track the age of the egg. A protocol that recruits fourteen follicles instead of eight gives the lab more material to sort through. It does not make the eggs younger.

This is why counseling that focuses on response, on estradiol levels and follicle counts and how many eggs were retrieved, can feel encouraging right up until the week it stops being encouraging. Those measures describe how the ovary answered the drugs. They do not describe what was inside.

## What cannot be predicted

There is no blood test taken beforehand that tells a 42-year-old whether she is in the 33 percent or the 67 percent.

AMH is often presented to women as a fertility score, and it is the number they remember and repeat and cry over. What it measures is the size of the antral follicle pool, which predicts how many eggs a stimulation is likely to yield. It is genuinely useful for choosing a drug dose. It does not measure egg quality, and it does not tell a woman whether this cycle will produce a normal embryo.

I almost never draw AMH outside of a specific reason to. It causes real distress and it rarely changes what I do next.

That uncertainty deserves to be said out loud in the consent conversation rather than discovered on the phone call. A woman can hold "we cannot predict this" when she is told in advance. Being told it afterward lands as something else entirely.

## The variable nobody offers to change

Age is the strongest driver in that dataset, and it is the one variable nobody can give back.

Which is why the years before a woman ever reaches a fertility clinic matter so much. Painful periods dismissed as normal in her teens. [Endometriosis](/endometriosis/) that takes years to name, and often only after several doctors. [PCOS](/pcos/) masked for a decade by a suppressive medication that hid the symptom rather than treating the disease, so that when she stops it in her thirties she discovers a condition that has been quietly progressing the whole time. A cycle chart nobody looked at. Twelve months of trying required before anyone will investigate, and then a work-up that establishes she is infertile without establishing why.

Every one of those delays is spent in the part of the curve that is still forgiving, and it delivers her to the clinic in the part that is not.

Diagnosing and treating the underlying disease early acts on the one variable that matters most in that dataset, which is time.

## The questions to ask

Before a retrieval, ask:

**"At this clinic, in women my age, what percentage of retrievals ended with no embryo available to transfer?"**

**"What percentage of tested cohorts came back with no normal embryo?"**

**"If this cycle produces nothing to transfer, what is the plan, and what will it cost?"**

And ask the question that should have been asked first, whenever it is asked: what is causing my infertility, and what has been ruled out. A woman is entitled to a name for her condition before she is asked to spend her eggs on going around it.
