In the space of about two months in early 2025, three separate research groups reported the same thing. There are microplastics in the fluid surrounding human eggs.

This is new. Until last year, nobody had looked.

### What was actually found

- **14 of 18 women** had microplastics in their follicular fluid, at an average of 2,191 particles per millilitre, in the first study to look
- **86.4 percent detection of polyethylene** specifically, in a second study of 44 women using a different measurement method
- **1,739 microplastic particles spanning 30 polymer types**, in a third study of 19 samples
- **The particles are small enough to matter.** Mean diameter around 4.5 micrometres in the first study, and in laboratory work the smaller beads crossed the zona pellucida and entered the egg
- **The three studies disagree about harm.** One found a negative correlation between polyethylene level and fertilization rate. Another found no correlation with fertilization, miscarriage, or live birth at all
- **In mouse oocytes, all seven bead types tested reduced maturation rates**
- **What the IVF dish and culture media contribute has not been measured**

That last line is the reason to write this article, and it is the one I want to be most careful about.

## The three studies

**Montano and colleagues, Salerno, Italy.** Eighteen women undergoing assisted reproduction. Using electron microscopy with an elemental detector, they found plastic particles under 10 micrometres in 14 of the 18 follicular fluid samples, averaging 2,191 particles per millilitre and ranging from none to 7,181. Mean particle diameter was about 4.5 micrometres.

They also looked for associations. Microplastic concentration correlated significantly with FSH. It correlated weakly and non-significantly with BMI, age, and estradiol. And it showed no correlation with AMH, with fertilization outcomes, with miscarriage, or with live birth.

**Wang and colleagues, Shanghai.** Forty-four women with infertility, follicular fluid measured by Raman microspectroscopy. Polyethylene was the most commonly detected polymer, in 86.4 percent of samples, and here the level did track with outcome: a negative correlation with fertilization rate, r = -0.407, P = 0.007.

Because a correlation in 44 women is a correlation in 44 women, they went to mice. Mice exposed to polyethylene produced fewer eggs (31.5 versus 36.3), fertilized at a lower rate (70.8 versus 85.2 percent), and had a higher proportion of poor-quality eggs (28.2 versus 16.5 percent). The proposed mechanism runs through disrupted metabolism in the follicular fluid, raised inflammation-related gene expression, and increased reactive oxygen species.

**Ni and colleagues, Nanjing.** Nineteen samples, laser direct infrared spectroscopy. Of 7,956 particles counted, 1,739 were microplastics, spanning 30 types. The most abundant were chlorinated polyethylene, fluorosilicone rubber, PVC, butadiene rubber, and styrene-butadiene-styrene. A separate quantification put polyethylene at 22.284 mg/kg, well above PVC, polypropylene, and polystyrene.

Then they tested seven bead types against oocyte maturation in the laboratory. Smaller beads penetrated the zona pellucida and entered the egg; larger ones stuck to the outside. All seven reduced maturation rates compared with control.

**One correction worth making, because I have seen this stated loosely, including in my own draft slides.** The maturation experiment in that third study was done on mouse oocytes, not human ones. "Every type tested impaired egg maturation" is true of mouse eggs in a dish. It is not a finding about human eggs in a woman, and saying it without the word mouse overstates it.

## What this does and does not establish

The detection is solid. Three groups, three countries, three different measurement technologies, same answer. Microplastics are in human follicular fluid. That part I would now consider established.

The harm is not established. One study found a fertilization signal and one explicitly did not, and both are small. The Salerno null result deserves as much airtime as the Shanghai correlation, and it usually gets less, because a null result does not make a headline. The mouse work is more consistent, and mice are exposed at doses chosen by the experimenter.

What we have is a real exposure, a plausible mechanism, animal data pointing one way, and human data that has not settled. That is an argument for urgency in research. It is not yet an argument for alarm in a clinic room, and I am not going to tell a woman that the plastic in her water bottle is why she is not pregnant. I do not know that, and neither does anyone else.

## The question nobody has asked

Here is what strikes me as a fertility surgeon rather than as an environmental scientist.

Every one of these studies measured what the woman brought with her. Her follicular fluid, aspirated at retrieval, carrying whatever her life had deposited in it.

In an IVF cycle, that fluid is not the end of the exposure. The egg is then removed and placed in a plastic dish, in commercial culture media, handled with plastic pipettes, and kept there for five or six days while the embryo forms. In the previous article in this series I wrote about what happens biologically during those days.

I went looking for the study that measures what those consumables shed into the culture environment, and what it does to the embryo. I could not find one. That is a negative search result and not a proof, and I want to be precise about the claim: leaching of plastic-derived compounds from laboratory plasticware is a well-established literature in other fields. What appears to be missing is the measurement inside clinical IVF, tied to embryo outcome.

If that study exists, I would genuinely like to be sent it, and this article will be corrected. If it does not exist, that is remarkable. We are now measuring microplastics in follicular fluid, semen, placenta, and blood, and the one compartment where a human embryo is deliberately and routinely housed in plastic for the most sensitive week of its development has not been characterized.

The question is answerable. It needs a laboratory, a mass spectrometer, and someone deciding it is worth asking.

## What to do with this, practically

For a woman reading this and worrying: nothing here means your exposure has ruined anything. The strongest human finding is a modest correlation in a small study, contradicted by another small study. Reducing plastic contact is reasonable on general health grounds and cheap to do, and I would not want anyone taking on a new source of guilt over it.

For a woman considering IVF: this belongs in the same conversation as everything else in this series. Not as a reason to refuse, but as one more area where the honest answer is that it has not been studied and you are entitled to know that.

For the field: the detection papers are done. The exposure is confirmed. The next study is obvious, and someone should run it.

## The pattern underneath

Restorative reproductive medicine keeps arriving at the same structural observation. The questions that get funded are the ones that improve a procedure. The questions about what the procedure does, over a longer horizon than a pregnancy test, are the ones that stay open for decades.

Looking for the cause of infertility and treating it is not a rejection of technology. It is a preference for the path where fewer of these unanswered questions apply at all.
