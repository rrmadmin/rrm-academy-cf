In 2013 the American Society for Reproductive Medicine removed the experimental label from egg freezing. In the same document, in the section on using it to delay childbearing, it wrote that **"marketing this technology for the purpose of deferring childbearing may give women false hope."**

That sentence is still there. The elective egg freezing market was built anyway.

### The short answer

- **ASRM's 2013 guideline said the data were insufficient to recommend elective egg freezing**, and warned that marketing it to defer childbearing may give women false hope
- **ACOG agreed the following year:** not enough data to recommend it for circumventing reproductive aging in healthy women
- **The current 2021 guideline still says there is insufficient evidence to predict live birth rates after planned egg freezing**
- **About 1 woman in 9 who freezes eggs ever returns to use them**, a pooled return rate of 11.1% across ten studies, at an average freezing age of 37
- **The guideline that approved it devotes two sentences to what the procedure does to the woman.** The words complication, torsion, bleeding and anesthesia do not appear in it
- **AMH, the test that usually prompts the decision, did not predict who conceived** in 750 women without an infertility history

Everything below is sourced from those documents and studies, with citations at the end.

## The version most women hear

She is thirty-four, or thirty-seven, and someone has told her that she has options now. A colleague did it. An influencer she follows did it and posted the whole thing. Her employer may even cover it.

The pitch is coherent and it is kind in tone. Freeze now, while your eggs are younger. Buy yourself time. Take the pressure off.

Underneath the pitch is a promise that nobody quite states out loud: that this will work if she needs it.

## What does the ASRM egg freezing guideline actually say?

In 2013, the Practice Committees of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine and the Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology published the guideline that removed the experimental label from egg freezing. It is the document the entire industry rests on. It is seven pages long and anyone can read it.

Its stated basis for lifting the label was that fertilization and pregnancy rates with vitrified and warmed eggs were similar to fresh eggs in young women, and that the limited available data on offspring showed no increase in chromosomal abnormalities or birth defects. That is a statement about how the eggs performed in a laboratory and about the babies who resulted.

Then, in the section on using it to defer childbearing, the same guideline says this:

> "Data on the safety, efficacy, cost-effectiveness, and emotional risks of elective oocyte cryopreservation are insufficient to recommend elective oocyte cryopreservation. Marketing this technology for the purpose of deferring childbearing may give women false hope and encourage women to delay childbearing."

And in its formal recommendations:

> "There are not yet sufficient data to recommend oocyte cryopreservation for the sole purpose of circumventing reproductive aging in healthy women."

The following year the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists endorsed that guideline and repeated the caution in its own words: there are not yet sufficient data to recommend egg freezing for the sole purpose of circumventing reproductive aging in healthy women.

In 2018 ASRM's own Ethics Committee restated that women considering it must be informed about the uncertainty in both its efficacy and its long-term effects.

The phrase *false hope* is not mine. It is theirs, in the guideline, in the section about the exact use the procedure is now sold for.

## Has the guidance changed since 2013?

The 2013 guideline was superseded in 2021. The current one states plainly that "there is insufficient evidence to predict live birth rates after planned OC." It does add that on limited data, live birth rates appear better for women who freeze younger rather than older.

Twelve years after the label came off, the field's own position is still that it cannot tell a woman what her chances are.

## What does the guideline say about risks to the woman?

The 2013 guideline states in its opening that it covers the technology, the clinical outcomes, and the risks. Its risk section is substantial on offspring: birth weight, congenital anomalies, chromosomal findings, developmental follow-up.

On what the procedure does to the woman, it says two sentences. That the well-described risks of ovarian stimulation and egg retrieval apply, and that because no embryo is being transferred, the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome is very low.

The second sentence is correct. A cycle with no transfer does carry less hyperstimulation risk, because there is no pregnancy driving the late form of it.

The first sentence is a cross-reference with no number attached. And across the whole document, the words complication, torsion, bleeding, and anesthesia do not appear. Infection appears four times, each time about contaminated liquid nitrogen and the safety of the stored eggs.

A woman reading that guideline to find out what the procedure might do to her body will not find it there.

## How many women actually use their frozen eggs?

The most useful figure in this conversation is how many women ever come back.

A 2024 systematic review pooled ten separate studies of women who froze eggs to preserve future fertility. Their average age at freezing was 37.2 years.

**The pooled return rate was 11.1 percent.**

About one woman in nine.

There are many reasons a woman does not come back, and several of them are happy ones. She conceived on her own. She decided she did not want children after all. Her circumstances changed. The other eight did not fail. But every success rate she was quoted is conditional on a step most women never take, and nobody told her that.

## Does AMH predict whether you will need frozen eggs?

Most women decide to freeze after an AMH result.

AMH is presented as a fertility score, and it is the number women remember and repeat and worry about at two in the morning. What it actually measures is the size of the pool of follicles available in a given cycle, which predicts how many eggs a stimulation is likely to produce. That is a genuinely useful number, and it is the clinic's question rather than hers.

In 2017, a prospective study followed 750 women aged 30 to 44 with no history of infertility who were trying to conceive. Women with low AMH had a 65 percent probability of conceiving within six cycles. Women with normal AMH had 62 percent. By twelve cycles it was 84 percent against 75 percent. None of those differences was statistically significant, and the authors concluded that the findings do not support using AMH to assess natural fertility in women like these.

Low AMH did not mean a woman was running out of time to conceive. It meant she would probably yield fewer eggs in a stimulated cycle.

Those are different facts, and only one of them is the reason she is in the room.

## Are IVF pregnancies higher risk?

Every number so far stops at a live birth, as though that were the end of the story. It is the beginning of a pregnancy, and those pregnancies have been studied.

The Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine recommends a detailed fetal anatomy ultrasound for every pregnancy conceived through IVF or ICSI. That examination is defined elsewhere in the specialty's own guidance as an indication-driven study for patients with known risk factors, and one that is not intended to be performed routinely in all pregnancies. The same document recommends weekly fetal surveillance from 36 weeks.

The pregnancy is managed as higher risk. The woman is generally not told that is what is happening, or why.

To be careful about what that means: much of the crude difference between IVF pregnancies and naturally conceived ones is explained by the underlying subfertility rather than the procedure, and when researchers compare a woman's IVF-conceived child against her own naturally conceived child, a good deal of the apparent excess falls away. What does not fall away is the part that tracks the procedure itself. In a sibling cohort of 4.5 million singletons, fresh transfers carried a small birthweight deficit, a higher rate of small-for-gestational-age babies, and a higher preterm rate, while frozen transfers went the other direction toward larger babies. Those differences follow the transfer method rather than the mother, which is why subfertility cannot account for them.

A woman weighing egg freezing is being asked to evaluate a procedure whose payoff, if it ever comes, is a pregnancy of this kind. She is entitled to that in the same conversation.

## What do egg freezing success rates actually depend on?

The pitch implies this is a product with a known success rate that you buy from a good clinic. The clinic is one of the smaller variables.

What these eggs turn out to be worth depends on **her age**, on **her health**, including conditions nobody has looked for yet, on what develops in her body between now and the day she uses them, and, if there is a partner, on **his sperm**, which is half of the equation and usually goes untested until years later.

The cost is not one cycle either. It is storage every year, often more than one cycle to bank enough eggs at thirty-seven, and the real possibility that when the time comes the two of them decide against IVF anyway. Because of the cost. Because of what it involves. Because of what they have learned by then about the risks.

That last outcome is a couple making an informed decision they were not equipped to make the first time.

## Is egg freezing worth it for a healthy woman?

Egg freezing helps some women. For a woman facing chemotherapy or surgery that will take her ovaries, it is a genuine good and the entire calculation is different.

For a healthy woman with no diagnosed fertility problem, here is what the evidence supports. Her professional societies said the data were insufficient to recommend it for this purpose and warned that marketing it this way might give false hope. Twelve years later they still cannot predict her live birth rate. About one woman in nine returns to use what she stored. The test that prompted the decision does not predict whether she will need it. The procedure carries real risks to her body that its founding guideline covered in two sentences. And if it works, it works by producing a pregnancy the specialty watches more closely than others.

She is being shown one side of this. The counseling comes from an industry that earns when she says yes.

## What should you ask before freezing your eggs?

The question to ask is not what this clinic's numbers are.

It is this: **what is actually going on with my health, and with his, and can any of it be found and treated now?**

A woman freezing eggs at thirty-seven because she is worried about her fertility may have something diagnosable and treatable sitting there today. Endometriosis. [PCOS](/pcos/), now officially renamed PMOS. Thyroid disease. [Chronic endometritis](/endometritis/). A luteal phase problem. Her partner may have a semen abnormality that is itself a marker of his broader health and that nobody has checked. Freezing eggs looks for none of it.

Finding one of those changes everything about what she should do next, and it changes it in her favor.

Time is what she is trying to buy. Answers are what would actually buy it.
