There is a week at the very beginning of a human life when the body does something it will never do again. It sets the length of its telomeres, and from that point forward they only shorten.

Most people assume a baby inherits its parents' wear. An older mother, an older father, a longer wait, and surely the child starts a little further along. That is not how it works, and the experiments that showed it are more than twenty years old.

### What is established

- **Telomere length is reset in the early embryo, not inherited.** In mice and cattle, an elongation program runs at the morula-to-blastocyst transition and pushes telomere length back toward a set point
- **The cleanest proof came from cloned embryos.** Their telomeres were restored to normal length regardless of how worn the donor nucleus was
- **In human embryos, telomeres lengthen by the blastocyst stage.** The two landmark papers disagree about the machinery, telomerase in one, recombination in the other, and agree on the window
- **Children conceived by IVF have been found to have shorter telomeres at one year old** than children conceived spontaneously, in a study of 1,137 people from 365 families
- **The association was strongest for blastocyst-stage transfer**, the technique that keeps the embryo in culture through exactly the days when the reset happens
- **In mice, in vitro culture suppressed telomerase activity at the early blastocyst stage** while leaving the late cleavage stage alone

That last finding is the one worth sitting with. The window in which an embryo rebuilds its telomeres, and the window in which an IVF embryo is living in a dish, are the same window.

## What the reset actually is

A telomere is the cap on the end of a chromosome. Every time a cell divides, the cap gets a little shorter, and when it runs down the cell stops dividing. That countdown is one of the better-understood clocks in human aging.

If the countdown simply carried over from parent to child, every generation would start shorter than the last, and after enough generations there would be nothing left. Something has to reset it.

In 2004, Schaetzlein and colleagues found the reset in mice and cattle. Telomeres lengthened sharply at the morula-to-blastocyst transition, and they lengthened toward a set point rather than by a fixed amount. Embryos that started short gained more. The experiment that settles it is the cloning one: take a nucleus from an adult animal, with adult telomeres, and the embryo built from it still arrives at normal length. The embryo is not reading its parents' clock. It is winding a new one.

In 2007, Liu and colleagues showed the same lengthening in early development and argued the mechanism was recombination rather than telomerase. The field has not fully settled which it is, and for the purposes of this article it does not need to. Both papers put the event in the same few days.

## Why this matters for a dish

A conventional IVF cycle now often uses blastocyst-stage transfer. The embryo stays in culture for five or six days and is placed in the uterus after the blastocyst has formed. Clinically there are good reasons for it, mostly around selecting which embryos are developing well.

It also means the reset happens outside the body.

In 2022, a group at Nanjing Medical University measured leukocyte telomere length using whole-genome sequencing in 1,137 people from 365 families, comparing 202 children conceived by ART with 205 conceived spontaneously. At one year old, the ART-conceived children had shorter telomeres, after adjusting for plurality, sex, and other factors. Broken down by technique, blastocyst-stage transfer carried the stronger association. They then repeated the measurement in 586 more ART-conceived children across five centers using two different methods, and the finding held.

Then they went to mice, where the population confound goes away. Blastocyst-stage transfer produced shorter telomeres at day one of life and still at six months. And in the experiment that connects the two halves of this article, culturing mouse embryos in vitro did not shorten telomeres at the late cleavage stage, but it did suppress telomerase activity at the early blastocyst stage.

## What this does not show

A careful reader should hold three things at once.

The human arm is observational. The families who use IVF differ from the families who do not, and they differ in exactly the ways that might independently affect a child's telomeres: they are older, and subfertility is itself associated with the underlying health problems that shorten telomeres. This is confounding by indication, and this study design cannot rule it out. A Norwegian cohort has since looked at telomere length against fecundability and ART use from the other direction, asking whether telomere length relates to needing ART at all, which is the pathway that would explain the association without the procedure doing anything.

The mouse arm is where the procedure gets isolated from the population, and it points the same way. That is meaningful, and mice are not people.

And nobody has shown that a shorter telomere at age one changes anything about that child's life. Telomere length is a marker of an aging-related process, not a diagnosis and not a destiny.

So the honest statement is this: the reset is real and well established, the overlap between the reset window and the culture window is not in dispute, and the human association is suggestive and unresolved. That is a reason to study it properly, which the authors of the Nature Medicine paper said themselves.

## The part that should not be controversial

A woman deciding between a day-three transfer and a day-five transfer is making a decision about which days of her embryo's development happen in a laboratory. She is usually told about implantation rates, and that is a real consideration. She is almost never told there is a published, replicated signal about telomere length attached to that same choice, or that the biology gives it a plausible mechanism.

She needs to be told, so that the choice is hers.

This is the shape of the pattern across nearly everything in this series. The information exists, it is in the field's own best journals, and it does not survive the trip to the consent conversation.

## What restorative medicine does differently here

This is an argument about sequence.

Restorative reproductive medicine starts by asking why conception is not happening, and treating what it finds. When that works, the first week of a life happens where it was always going to happen, and none of the questions in this article ever arise. When it does not work, IVF is still there, and the woman arrives at it healthier and better informed than she would have otherwise.

The reason to look for the cause first is not that the laboratory is sinister. It is that a diagnosis is worth having on its own, and a body that has been treated tends to do better either way.
