Between January 2021 and December 2024, about five million commercially insured Americans passed through a national claims database. A research team pulled out the patients who received an infertility diagnosis and later started IVF. Then they read the record backward and asked a simple question. How much of the evaluation that the major professional societies recommend had actually been done first?

Nine months after the infertility diagnosis, IVF use had climbed to between 70 and 85 percent. Many of the recommended evaluations and treatments were still sitting below 40 percent. Several stayed below 15.

The first author on that paper is a family physician in Cranbrook, British Columbia, named Tracey Parnell.

## Who is Tracey Parnell?

Tracey Parnell, MD, MRM, CCFP, is a board-certified Canadian physician and researcher. She serves as Director of Communications and Development on the IIRRM Board of Trustees. Her own IIRRM profile styles the role Global Director of Communications and Development. She is an Assistant Clinical Professor at the University of British Columbia. She is also a managing editor of the Journal of Restorative Reproductive Medicine.

Her training includes a Master of Reproductive Medicine with Excellence from the University of New South Wales. She also holds a certificate in Reproductive Health Research from the World Health Organization and Geneva University.

She keeps her own restorative reproductive medicine practice in Canada. She is also medical director of the RRM Education group, RRMed, which brings leading practitioners together to deliver advanced RRM training.

Four things at once: a rural practice, a university appointment, a journal, and a research record going back nearly twenty years.

## What is the IIRRM?

The International Institute for Restorative Reproductive Medicine began as a conversation. Physicians from Canada, Ireland and the United Kingdom met in November 2000 to discuss forming a professional body and a research institute. Colleagues from Australia and the United States joined the organizing meetings, and the IIRRM was formally created.

The founding vision was to unite everyone providing RRM care, whichever cycle charting method they taught their patients. The stated mission is to advance excellence in restorative reproductive medicine by supporting practice and research through mentorship, education and collaboration.

Today the IIRRM is a Charitable Incorporated Organisation registered in the United Kingdom. It is also a 501(c)(3) public charity in the United States, governed by a Council of Trustees. Its trustees practice in Canada, Ireland, the United States, Chile, Ukraine, Poland and the United Kingdom. It publishes the Journal of Restorative Reproductive Medicine, runs the STORRM research registry, and hosts the annual international Congress. Its journal clubs run in English, Spanish and Ukrainian.

Its members' work runs through the RRM Academy research library. Dr. Parnell co-authored the 2008 study of [NaProTechnology outcomes in an Irish general practice](https://rrmacademy.org/library/outcomes-from-treatment-of-infertility-with-natural-procreative-technology-in-an-recj7cwubt4vlyfjl/). She co-authored the 2018 Frontiers in Medicine study of [live births through RRM after failed IVF](https://rrmacademy.org/library/healthy-singleton-pregnancies-from-restorative-reproductive-medicine-rrm-after-f-recior3akxtg2a6ya/). She was second author on the [iNEST enrollment and methods paper](https://rrmacademy.org/library/international-natural-procreative-technology-evaluation-and-surveillance-of-trea-recudgdct40otosdm/), which set up prospective data collection across clinics in several countries.

## What is restorative reproductive medicine?

Restorative reproductive medicine finds the conditions underneath reproductive trouble and treats them, so the reproductive system can do its own work. The IIRRM puts it this way: RRM clinicians focus investigation and treatment on correcting abnormalities, rather than suppressing, destroying or bypassing normal function.

In practice that starts with information. A woman charts her own cycle, and that record becomes clinical data rather than a diary. RRM clinicians work at the level of the couple. Male factor is solely responsible in roughly one in five couples, and it contributes in many more. The couple is the patient.

This approach reads unexplained infertility as undiagnosed infertility. Something is causing it. Nobody has found it yet. If this is new to you, start with our guide to [what restorative reproductive medicine is](https://rrmacademy.org/what-is-rrm/).

## How did Tracey Parnell come to this work?

She has been publishing outcomes since 2008. That year the Journal of the American Board of Family Medicine ran her study of couples treated in an Irish general practice. The cohort was 1,072 couples who had been trying to conceive for at least a year. Average female age was 35.8. They had been trying for a mean of 5.6 years. A third had already attempted assisted reproductive technology. For couples completing up to 24 months of treatment, the life-table cumulative proportion of first live births was 52.8 per 100 couples. The crude proportion was 25.5.

Those two numbers measure different things. The life-table figure accounts for couples who leave treatment along the way. The crude figure divides live births by everyone who ever started.

Ten years later she co-authored a Frontiers in Medicine study of 403 patients. Every one of them came to RRM with previous IVF behind her. Their average age was 37.2. They carried an average of 5.8 years of infertility and 2.1 previous IVF attempts. Only 5 percent had ever had a live birth from IVF. The life-table live birth rate with RRM was 32.1 percent, crude 18.4 percent. The whole study population produced one twin pregnancy, and 92 percent of the babies arrived at 37 weeks or later.

Then she helped build the infrastructure. The iNEST paper in Human Reproduction Open described a prospective cohort collecting treatment data across clinics in several countries. Building a shared dataset like that is unglamorous, and it is what makes later evidence possible.

Nearly twenty years of a rural family practice in British Columbia, with a paper trail running the whole way through it.

## What gap is Tracey Parnell closing?

She is measuring what actually happens to women before IVF, at national scale, using the health system's own records.

Her 2026 paper in the Journal of Restorative Reproductive Medicine does exactly that, and she is its first author. Her team searched four years of commercial claims covering about five million insured members. They found the patients who went on to IVF. Then they checked which recommended evaluations and treatments appeared in the record first. By three months after diagnosis, 28 to 39 percent of patients had already started IVF, depending on the diagnosis. By nine months, IVF use reached 70 to 85 percent while many recommended steps stayed under 40 percent adherence.

Read that slowly, because it describes real women. A woman receives an infertility diagnosis. Within a few months she is in an IVF cycle. The system moved her past the workup that might have named her actual condition, the same workup the professional societies themselves recommend. IVF does not treat what caused her infertility. It goes around it. She is still infertile afterward, and she still does not know why.

Her second gap is definitional. In July 2026 the IIRRM published a consensus document with Dr. Parnell as second author. It proposes a definition. Infertility is a clinical condition, recognized by the symptom of an inability to conceive or to sustain a pregnancy. That symptom indicates underlying male or female pathology. You can read the [full consensus document](https://rrmacademy.org/library/definition-of-infertility-and-indications-for-fertility-evaluation-consensus-doc-tlh4azyr/) and [her care-gap analysis](https://rrmacademy.org/library/real-world-fertility-evaluation-care-prior-to-in-vitro-fertilization-care-gaps-t-phovc5ur/) in our library.

The definition matters more than it sounds. Define infertility by the symptom and the pathology, and the next step is a diagnosis. Define it by months of trying, and the next step is a procedure. The consensus process took the long road. The IIRRM invited around 2,500 individuals and 44 organizations across 92 countries to review the draft. The board approved it unanimously.

Her third gap is the workforce. There are not enough clinicians trained to do this, and RRMed exists to change that number.
