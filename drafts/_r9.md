Egg retrieval has risks documented with numbers, and risks nobody has ever measured. Women are usually handed the word rare in place of either. Of 34 influencer videos about egg freezing, five discussed physical risks and none mentioned hyperstimulation.

### The numbers that exist

- **Mild ovarian hyperstimulation: roughly 20 to 33 percent of stimulated cycles**, per ASRM's own guideline
- **Hospital admission for hyperstimulation: 1.2 percent of stimulated cycles**, across 186,168 cycles in a Danish national register. The rate did not fall across seventeen years
- **Serious complications from the retrieval: about 1 in 130 women**, and about 1 in 180 admitted to hospital, in 12,615 women across 23,827 consecutive retrievals. Milder problems were never counted
- **Complications were significantly more common with less experienced operators**, in that same dataset
- **Damage to the surface of the ovary happens every time.** The medical literature calls it inevitable, and classifies it as part of the procedure rather than as a complication
- **Where a retrieval was complicated by abscess, the next cycle produced about 30 percent fewer eggs**
- **Pain rose measurably** across stimulation and retrieval in women without endometriosis, on validated scales
- **Your own risk sits above or below the average.** Hyperstimulation risk rises with the number of eggs retrieved, with a pregnancy in that cycle, and with an ovulatory disorder diagnosis, usually PCOS
- **Retrieval complication risk rises separately:** more eggs, an operator under 250 retrievals, younger age and lower body weight, and previous pelvic surgery or infection

### The questions nobody has answered

- **How often does retrieval leave scarring on an ovary?** Unknown. The second-look study done for a similar procedure has never been done for egg retrieval
- **Does repeated stimulation reduce ovarian reserve?** More than three cycles is associated with faster decline, direction unresolved
- **What happens to egg donors long term?** There is no systematic follow-up. None
- **Does the tenfold estrogen exposure worsen endometriosis?** Unsettled, and the studies mostly stopped measuring on retrieval day

The rest of this article is where each of those numbers comes from.

## Why "rare" is not an answer to a question about risk

The word rare does a lot of work in this conversation. Rarely, there is bleeding. Rarely, infection. Rarely, a serious reaction to the medication.

Rare is not a number. It is a feeling about a number, offered in place of one, and a woman signing a form is entitled to the number.

Some of these numbers exist and are published. Others have never been produced, for questions that are entirely answerable. The second group is the more interesting one, and it is almost never mentioned at all.

Here is both.

## Part one: what is documented

### How common is ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome?

Mild ovarian hyperstimulation appears in roughly 20 to 33 percent of stimulated cycles, according to the American Society for Reproductive Medicine's own guideline on preventing it. Moderate to severe runs somewhere around 0.5 to 1.5 percent under modern protocols.

The form that puts a woman in hospital is the one worth pinning down, and a national register did it. Every woman admitted to a Danish hospital with ovarian hyperstimulation between 2001 and 2017: 2,261 admissions across 186,168 stimulated cycles, or **1.2 percent**. Three quarters of them stayed longer than 24 hours. Average stay was 4.3 days for the early form and 6.2 days for the late form.

The part that should give everyone pause is that the annual rate did not fall. It moved between 0.9 and 1.4 percent for seventeen years, with no significant change, in a country with excellent records.

A United States surveillance system counting coded diagnoses reports a much lower figure, 0.53 percent by 2015, and a threefold decline since 2006. Both numbers are real. They come from different systems counting different things, one register counting hospital admissions and one surveillance program counting diagnostic codes. Which one a woman is quoted depends on who is quoting.

### What can go wrong during the retrieval?

A program that performed 23,827 consecutive retrievals in 12,615 women, over twenty years, published what it found.

**Counted per woman rather than per procedure, 0.76 percent had a complication and 0.56 percent were admitted to hospital.** That is roughly one woman in 130, and one in 180 admitted, with an average stay of just under three days.

Both figures matter and they are not the same. Per retrieval the rate is 0.4 percent. Per woman it is nearly double, because a woman is not a procedure. She is a person who may go through this two or three times, and the per-woman figure is the one that describes what she is taking on.

### Why these are the serious complications, not all of them

That study was a look back through one clinic's records. A records review finds what somebody wrote down at the time. The paper's own threshold for calling something severe was admission to hospital for at least 24 hours.

So a woman who went home and had days of pain, or bleeding that frightened her, or a fever she rode out, and who never rang the clinic about it, is not in that number. Nobody has ever taken a group of women after retrieval and simply asked them what happened over the following month.

**These are the complications serious enough to reach a doctor and be written down.** They are a floor, not a count. The same pattern shows up in how clinics describe risk. An analysis of fertility clinic websites found they foreground rare extreme risks such as severe hyperstimulation while omitting frequent ordinary ones such as bloating.

### Does it matter who performs the retrieval?

Buried in that same paper is something no one quotes. Complications were significantly more common when the doctor had performed fewer than 250 retrievals. Against that group, an operator past 250 carried an odds ratio of 0.63 (95% CI 0.40 to 0.99).

Which means the 0.76 percent is an average across operators of very different experience, and a woman's own risk sits either side of it depending on who is in the room. She is entitled to ask which.

The other risk factors that study identified are in the next section, because they are the ones a woman can actually use.

### What is never counted at all?

In 2001, Fertility and Sterility published a study on blood loss after uncomplicated retrievals. Its opening description of the procedure is the most useful sentence I have found on this subject:

> "The procedure, which involves puncture then aspiration of ovarian follicles, inevitably results in damage to the fine vascular network of blood vessels on the ovarian surface and theca interna layer, and tends to produce varying degrees of blood loss."

Inevitably. And then the same paper defines what counts as an uncomplicated retrieval: one that does not injure the iliac vessels, damage pelvic organs, or cause pelvic infection.

Read those two sentences together. Damage to the surface of the ovary is described as unavoidable, and the definition of a normal, uncomplicated retrieval already includes it. Which means the field classifies that damage as the procedure, not as a complication of it.

That is precisely why it appears in no outcome table anywhere. Nothing that is part of the intervention by definition gets counted as a harm of it.

### Infection, bleeding, torsion

These are documented in women, in published case reports spanning three decades. Ovarian abscess after the needle passed through an endometrioma. Pelvic abscess presenting late, in women with severe endometriosis. Ruptured tubo-ovarian abscess with tissue findings. Bilateral ovarian abscesses requiring drainage. Abdominal sepsis. Hemoperitoneum. Ovarian torsion presenting at seventeen weeks of a twin pregnancy.

Individually these are uncommon events. Collectively they are proof that the mechanism operates.

And there is one study showing the damage lasts. Fifteen women whose retrieval was complicated by a tubo-ovarian abscess were compared against their own next cycle within a year. The number of eggs retrieved fell from 8.1 to 5.4, which the authors describe as **a 30 percent reduction in ovarian response**. Their conclusion was that abscess after fertility treatment has a detrimental effect on ovarian function.

Fifteen women is a small study. It is also a within-woman comparison, which controls for a great deal, and it is directly on the point.

### Does an egg retrieval hurt?

A prospective study at Lille University Hospital followed 278 patients through IVF, measuring pain on validated scales at three points: before treatment, at the end of stimulation, and on the day of retrieval.

In the women **without** endometriosis, pain rose significantly across those three points.

The same study found, before treatment even started, that 22 percent of the endometriosis patients had suspected or confirmed depression and 33 percent had anxiety, against 8 and 20 percent in the women without it.

## What raises your own risk

Population averages are not much use to the person reading them. These are the factors the published studies actually identified, so a woman can work out roughly where she sits.

### Things that raise the risk of hyperstimulation

From a United States surveillance analysis of IVF cycles, the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation was:

- **Nearly four times higher when more than 30 eggs were retrieved**
- **About three times higher in cycles that resulted in a pregnancy**, which is what drives the later-onset form
- **About two and a half times higher in women whose infertility diagnosis was an ovulatory disorder**, which in practice usually means polycystic ovary syndrome, now officially renamed PMOS
- Slightly higher with uterine or tubal factor diagnoses

The researchers named the factors that can actually be changed: the number of eggs retrieved, whether a fresh transfer results in pregnancy, and which medication is used to suppress the pituitary during stimulation.

### Things that raise the risk of a complication from the retrieval

From the 23,827-retrieval series:

- **A doctor who has performed fewer than 250 retrievals**, compared with one who has done more than 250
- **A high number of eggs retrieved**
- A long procedure, and a long average time per egg
- **Being younger, with a lower body weight**
- **Previous abdominal or pelvic surgery, or previous pelvic infection**

And from the case reports, a specific one: having an **ovarian endometrioma** that the needle may pass through on its way to a follicle.

### Read those two lists together

**The number of eggs retrieved is on both.** It is the single biggest changeable predictor of hyperstimulation, and it also predicts complications from the retrieval itself.

Now consider what egg freezing is for. The entire point is to bank as many eggs as possible, and women who freeze at thirty-seven are routinely advised to come back for a second or third round to reach a target number.

So the thing she is being encouraged to maximize is the thing that raises her risk. Not because anyone intends her harm, but because that is how the incentive is built.

The second overlap is worth sitting with too. Elective egg freezing is marketed to young, healthy, often slim women. Younger age and lower body weight are on the complication list. And women with polycystic ovary syndrome, who tend to produce very high egg numbers, carry both the raised hyperstimulation risk from their diagnosis and the raised risk that comes with the egg count itself.

### A caution about how to use these numbers

These are multipliers, not her actual risk. They tell you a factor makes something more likely relative to a woman without it. To know what that means in absolute terms you need the baseline, which is roughly one percent of stimulated cycles ending in hospital admission for hyperstimulation, and roughly three quarters of one percent of women having a serious complication from the retrieval.

Take both numbers to whoever is proposing the cycle and ask them to work it out with your own egg count, your own diagnosis, and their own figures. A multiplier from one study and a baseline from another do not combine into your risk.

### Does hyperstimulation have effects beyond the acute illness?

The same surveillance analysis found that when hyperstimulation happened, the pregnancy that followed was more likely to end in a low birth weight baby and more likely to be preterm.

That is worth knowing, because hyperstimulation is often described as unpleasant but temporary. In this dataset it was not only temporary.

## Part two: what nobody has measured

Everything below is a question with a findable answer that nobody has found.

### Does egg retrieval cause ovarian scarring?

Ovaries scar when their surface is breached. We know this precisely. Ninety-six women with polycystic ovary syndrome, now also called PMOS, had their ovaries punctured during ovarian drilling and went back for a second look a short time later. **Adhesions had formed in 60 percent of the women and 46 percent of the treated ovaries**, and the number of punctures made no difference.

That procedure uses heat, and a retrieval needle does not, so the figure does not transfer directly. What it establishes is that the ovary is an organ that scars readily when you go through its surface.

Retrieval goes through its surface. Retrieval also causes bleeding and, sometimes, infection, and those are the two classic routes to adhesion formation in any pelvic surgery.

So: how often does a retrieval leave adhesions on a woman's ovary?

**Nobody knows, because nobody has looked.** The exact study that was done for ovarian drilling, take a consecutive series of women back for a second look and count, has never been done after egg retrieval. Not once, in a procedure performed hundreds of thousands of times a year.

Surgeons do see adhesions at laparoscopy in women who have had IVF. That observation does not prove the retrieval caused them. Women who have IVF are already more likely to have endometriosis, prior surgery, or prior infection, and all of those cause adhesions too. That is exactly why the second-look study is the one that would settle it. Its absence is the finding.

The only place anyone has directly examined ovaries after repeated needle aspiration is in horses. Thirty-two mares, 3,202 follicle punctures, ovaries removed and examined: no adhesions, minimal changes on histology.

That study belongs in this article, and so do its two limits, because they matter.

The published report does not say **how long after the last puncture the ovaries were examined.** For a study about scarring that is the detail everything turns on. Adhesions form within weeks, which is why the ovarian drilling study used a short-interval second look. Ovaries examined the same week as the final aspiration would show very little regardless.

And a mare's ovary is not built like a woman's. The equine ovary is described in the anatomical literature as having an inverted arrangement, with the cortex and medulla the opposite way round from ours, and ovulation occurring through a single discrete site called the ovulation fossa. In a woman, the follicle-bearing cortex is on the outside, and a retrieval needle crosses the capsule and that cortex on every pass. In a mare it does not. The needle is not going through the same tissue.

What does not explain the negative result is horses being resistant to adhesions. They are not. Adhesions after abdominal surgery are a well-recognized and serious problem in equine practice.

So the honest status of that study is an animal experiment of limited transferability, with the timing unstated, in an organ built the other way round. It remains the only direct look anyone has taken. That is the point worth sitting with: the reason we have it at all is that you can remove a mare's ovaries and examine them, and nobody does that for women.

### Do repeated cycles reduce ovarian reserve?

A 2025 study of 4,245 infertile women with repeated AMH measurements found that having **more than three ovarian stimulation cycles** was independently associated with a faster rate of AMH decline. The authors conclude that their results "highlight the need for caution regarding the potential impairment of ovarian reserve due to repeated cycles of ovarian stimulation."

The honest reading has a hole in it. Women who go through more than three cycles are women whose earlier cycles did not work, and cycles not working is what declining ovarian reserve looks like. The arrow could run either way and a retrospective study does not settle it.

So the accurate statement is that repeated stimulation is associated with faster loss of measured ovarian reserve, that the field's own researchers are urging caution about it, and that the study that would establish direction has not been done.

### Does retrieval provoke an immune response against the ovaries?

This one is often raised and the evidence is more interesting than the claim.

Researchers measured anti-ovarian antibodies before stimulation, at the end of stimulation, and after retrieval. Within an IVF cycle, **neither the stimulation nor the retrieval raised antibody levels.** IVF patients did carry higher levels than fertile women overall, and the elevated levels sat with women who had undergone five to eight prior cycles of ovulation induction.

The evidence implicates repeated ovulation induction, not the retrieval. Ovulation induction is the least examined intervention in this field, and the most casually prescribed.

### Does stimulation worsen endometriosis?

Ovarian stimulation raises estrogen to roughly **ten times** the level of a natural cycle. That figure comes from a review by IVF clinicians in Human Reproduction, not from critics.

Endometriosis responds to estrogen. Whether that exposure worsens the disease is unsettled. Reviews have been broadly reassuring about IVF and endometriosis pain, and those reviews deserve to be read. They also rest partly on very low quality evidence for the deepest form of the disease, and the studies underneath them mostly stopped measuring on the day of the retrieval.

A 2026 survey of 546 women with confirmed endometriosis found **48.9 percent reported their pelvic pain was worse after IVF.** The strongest predictor was a flare in the weeks immediately after the cycle, which is the window those earlier studies did not cover.

Nobody has shown that stimulation spreads endometriosis, and I am not saying it does. What can be said is that a woman with undiagnosed endometriosis receives a very large dose of the hormone her disease responds to, from a process that does not look for the disease, and the question of what that does to her over years has not been answered.

### What happens to egg donors long term?

Egg donors undergo more stimulation cycles and more retrievals than anyone else, at younger ages, for someone else's benefit. They are the group whose long-term outcomes would tell us the most.

**There is no systematic long-term follow-up of egg donors.** A physician whose daughter died of colon cancer after donating wrote a paper in Fertility and Sterility calling for exactly that follow-up. That was in 2008.

## Is egg retrieval less invasive than diagnostic laparoscopy?

Diagnostic laparoscopy is often described to women as the more serious of the two procedures, and egg retrieval as the minor one.

Each one crosses a wall to get in. Laparoscopy goes through the abdominal wall, retrieval through the wall of the vagina. Neither of those is an organ, and crossing them is not the difference between the two procedures.

The difference is what happens next.

Laparoscopy looks. It reaches an organ only when something has gone wrong, and in a Finnish national analysis of 70,607 procedures that happened in roughly 1.3 of every 1,000 cases: bowel, ureter, bladder, a blood vessel. Every one of those was a reportable complication, recorded and sent to a national injury body.

Retrieval puts a needle into an ovary. Not by accident, and not rarely. In 1,000 of every 1,000 cases, because that is the procedure. And it is counted nowhere.

In the same Finnish data, diagnostic laparoscopy's major complication rate sat below 0.6 per 1,000. Retrieval's hospital admission rate alone is 2.9 per 1,000. Those two figures are not defined identically and should not be presented as a head-to-head, but no published figure supports the reverse, and no study has ever been designed to compare them properly.

There is also a difference that has nothing to do with risk. Laparoscopy can answer the question of why a woman is not conceiving. Retrieval does not ask it. She leaves one with a diagnosis and the other with eggs.

## What a woman is entitled to be told

A woman is entitled to be told that mild hyperstimulation happens in up to a third of cycles, that about one retrieval in 250 produces a complication, that hospital admission for hyperstimulation has not fallen in seventeen years of national data, and that damage to the surface of her ovary is described in the medical literature as unavoidable and is counted as part of the procedure rather than as a harm of it.

She is also entitled to be told what is not known. That nobody has counted how often retrieval leaves scarring on an ovary. That the effect of repeated cycles on her ovarian reserve is associated but unresolved. That the women who undergo the most retrievals have never been followed.

Every one of those is an answerable question about a procedure performed hundreds of thousands of times each year. The studies have simply not been done.

Unfortunately, a woman cannot be told her risk, because nobody measured it. That sentence should appear on the consent form, and it does not appear anywhere.

## What to ask before an egg retrieval

**"What is your complication rate for retrievals, and how do you define a complication?"**

**"How many women at this clinic were admitted to hospital after a cycle last year?"**

**"How many retrievals have you personally performed?"** The published threshold in the largest series was 250.

**"How many eggs are you aiming for, and does that number change my risk?"**

**"If I do three cycles instead of one, what changes about my risk?"**

**"What is being done to find out why I am not conceiving, and when does that happen?"**

That last one is the question underneath all of it.

## What restorative medicine does differently here

This is an argument about sequence.

Everything above is the price of a procedure. None of it tells you what the procedure is for, and that is the part usually left out. A retrieval does not ask why you are not conceiving. It works around the question.

Restorative reproductive medicine asks it first. It looks for the endometriosis, the ovulation disorder, the thyroid problem, the chronic endometritis, and it treats what it finds. Sometimes that is enough on its own. When it is not, IVF is still there, and a woman arrives at it having already accepted these risks for a reason she understands rather than in place of an answer.

The point is not that nobody should ever have a retrieval. It is that a woman accepting a one in 130 chance of a serious complication deserves to know whether anyone tried to make it unnecessary first.
